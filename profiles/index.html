HW 4 · Steady moisture profiles above a water table

Setup

hA = −30 cm, λ = 1.2, Ks = 3.4 × 10−4 cm/s, c = 3 + 2/λ = 4.67, water table 80 cm below the surface.

With z the height above the water table and h the pressure head, Darcy's law q = −K(dh/dz + 1) is integrated up from h = 0 at the water table, using ΘE = (h/hA)−λ and K = KsΘEc. Where |h| < |hA| (the tension-saturated zone) ΘE = 1, so the answer is min(ΘE, 1). Upward q is positive.

Fluxes: q = −½Ks and −¼Ks (percolation), 0 (hydrostatic), and ½K(L) and 0.97K(L) (evaporation), where K(L) = Ks(L/−hA)−λc = 1.40 × 10−6 cm/s with L = 80 cm.

Shapes

Every curve starts saturated at the water table and stays at ΘE = 1 until the suction reaches |hA|. At rest that is 30 cm, and above it ΘE = (z/30)−1.2, falling to 0.31 at the surface. Percolation curves are wetter than the hydrostatic one and turn vertical where K = |q|. Evaporation curves are drier, and the gap grows toward the surface.

Why percolation and capillary rise differ

In steady flow, three forces act on the water: gravity (down), the suction gradient (pulling water toward drier soil), and friction with the grains, which always opposes the flow. With s = −h, Darcy's law becomes ds/dz = 1 + q/K.

  • At rest there is no friction, so suction alone holds the water up: s = z.
  • Percolation (water moving down): friction points up and carries part of the weight, so suction builds more slowly and the soil stays wetter. Where K drops to |q|, friction carries all of the weight. The suction gradient goes to zero and ΘE stops changing, at Θ* = (|q|/Ks)1/c = 0.86 and 0.74.
  • Capillary rise (water moving up): friction points down and adds to gravity, so suction must rise faster. Drier soil has a much lower K, which needs still more suction, so the soil dries quickly near the surface.

The notes expect ΘE ≈ 0 at the surface for 0.97K(L). The exact solution gives 0.22, because the largest flux the soil can lift to the surface is about 1.33K(L), not K(L). The surface only dries completely at that limit.

Sensitivity to soil texture

  • Air-entry suction |hA| sets how tall the saturated zone is. Finer soils have smaller pores and a larger |hA| (about 12 cm for sand, 40 cm for clay), so they stay saturated higher above the water table.
  • λ sets how fast ΘE falls above that zone. This soil's λ = 1.2 means fairly uniform pores, so it drains sharply. Finer, less sorted soils have a smaller λ and stay much wetter.
  • c sets the percolation limit Θ* = (|q|/Ks)1/c. Fine soils have a large c, so they need very little drying to lower K to |q|, and they stay nearly saturated while draining.
  • Capillary rise: coarse soils lose conductivity quickly as they dry, so they can supply little evaporation from a deep water table. Finer soils keep K over a wider suction range and lift water from farther down.
